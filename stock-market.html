Owners vs. price-setters

Who holds US equities (stocks), and who actually moves their prices

Foreign investors own about as much of the US stock market as America’s wealthiest 1%, and the bottom half of households own just 0.4% of it.

On a typical day, only about 1.7% of the market's value changes hands. That thin slice sets the price for everything else. Source: S&P 500, FRED, St. Louis Fed.

“In the short run, the market is a voting machine but in the long run it is a weighing machine.”

Benjamin Graham, as quoted by Warren Buffett in his letters to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders

Equity (stock) ownership is highly concentrated

Share of the total value of publicly traded US stock, with US households lined up from richest to poorest.

  • Foreign investors, based outside the US, own about 34%
  • The wealthiest 1% of US households own about 32%
  • The next wealthiest 9% own about 23%
  • The middle 40% own about 7%
  • Nonprofits, endowments and insurers own about 4%
  • The remaining US households, the least wealthy half, own about 0.4%

Foreign and institutional shares: Tax Policy Center, 2022 data. Household groups apply the Federal Reserve's Q2 2026 wealth split to the roughly 62% of publicly traded stock that US households own. Two sources are combined, so treat the shares as approximate. Counting private companies too, foreign investors hold about 42% of all US stock, up from about 25% in 2015. In plain terms: foreign investors own about as much of the US market as America's wealthiest 1%, while half of US households together own less than half a cent of every dollar, even though 58% of families own at least some stock.

Daily turnover: about 1.7% of market value

Turnover is the share of the market that changes hands. On any given day, almost all stock sits still, yet the last trade reprices every share.

So how much do 401(k)s own?

Zooming in on the roughly 62% of US stock that American households own, split by the kind of account it sits in.

  • Retirement accounts: 401(k)s, IRAs and pensions hold about 55% (roughly a third of the whole market)
  • Regular brokerage and fund accounts hold about 45% (roughly 28% of the whole market)

Tax Policy Center (Rosenthal and Mucciolo), end-2022 data for publicly traded stock. Retirement accounts are a big piece, but not an everyday-worker piece: about 54% of US households have a 401(k)-type plan or IRA, and their median balance is about $87,000 while the average is about $334,000, because a smaller number of very large accounts pull the average up (Congressional Research Service, 2022). Most of these retirement dollars belong to the wealthiest 10% of households.

The wealth effect: the top 10% drive half of consumer demand

When rising stock prices make people feel richer, they spend more; that is the wealth effect. So who keeps company revenues, and therefore earnings, growing?

  • The highest-earning 10% of households do about 50% of all spending
  • All other households (the lower-earning 90%) do the other 50% combined

Ranked by yearly income, not net worth (Moody's Analytics, 2025). This feeds a loop: stock gains make wealthy households feel richer, they spend more, earnings rise, and stocks rise again. The loop also runs in reverse.

Liquidity provision: algorithms do about half the trading

Liquidity means you can buy or sell quickly at a fair price. Market-making firms provide it by always standing ready to trade, mostly by computer, and they end each day owning almost nothing.

Zoom out: the noise fades, the earnings remain

Up close, the market looks like chaos. Step back far enough and prices rise along with what companies actually earn. In Graham’s terms: a voting machine day to day, a weighing machine over decades.

    The takeaway: a sticky base, a thin marginal layer

    US equities (stocks) rest on a concentrated, long-term holder base of wealthy households, retirement accounts and foreign investors that rarely trades. Prices are set at the margin, meaning by the last buyers and sellers, through a thin layer of trading. Roughly half of it comes from market-making algorithms that end each day nearly flat, holding almost no shares. That is how trillions of dollars in market value can move in a single day while ownership barely shifts.
