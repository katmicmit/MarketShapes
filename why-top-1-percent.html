The short answer

At the very top, wealth mostly is stock: founder stakes, private businesses and executive equity. The wealthiest households also save far more of their income and invest it, while most families hold their wealth in homes and pensions. And since 1989, stocks have grown faster than wages and home prices.

The top 1% share has not risen in a straight line. It reached about 51% in the mid-1990s, fell to around 40% after the dot-com crash, climbed to a peak of 52.3% in 2019, and has been above 50% again since 2024. Over the same period, the middle group lost about six points.

What keeps it in place

  • Uneven access to retirement plans. In 2026, 72% of private-sector workers could join an employer plan, but only 52% did. Access is far lower in low-wage and part-time jobs, and a workplace plan is how most families end up owning stock at all.
  • Falling interest rates. A Federal Reserve economist found that lower interest rates and corporate tax rates explain over 40% of real profit growth from 1989 to 2019. Lower rates raise stock values, and the benefit goes to whoever already owns the most stock.
  • Tax rules that reward holding. Gains are taxed only when stock is sold, and gains on assets held until death are erased for heirs. Unrealized gains make up about 41% of the top 1%'s wealth, so large portfolios tend to be held for decades and passed on whole.
  • Inheritance. Inheritances are even more concentrated than wealth itself. Fed survey data show the top 1% have received inheritances averaging about $719,000, compared with about $9,700 for the bottom half.
  • Compounding. A very large portfolio returning around 7% a year adds more in dollars than most households earn in wages. Once concentration exists, it reinforces itself unless a market crash breaks it.

What this figure does and doesn't measure

Many families own a little. In the Fed's 2022 survey, a record 58% of families held some stock, directly or indirectly. Participation is broad; the value is concentrated.

401(k)s are left out. The Fed counts stock inside 401(k)-type plans as pension wealth, not as stock. Since that is where middle-class stock ownership sits, the 50.9% figure overstates the top's share. A measure that includes those holdings put the top 1% at about 40% in 2016.

It covers households only. Foreign investors, pension funds and other institutions also own US stocks, so this is a share of household-held stock, not of the entire market.

It includes private companies as well as publicly traded shares, and it moves with the market, so small quarterly changes are not meaningful.
